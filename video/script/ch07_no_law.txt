Under Southern law, an enslaved person was property. They couldn't testify in court against a white person. They couldn't legally defend themselves against their owner.

In 1830, a North Carolina judge named Thomas Ruffin summed it up in one sentence: "The power of the master must be absolute, to render the submission of the slave perfect."

Absolute power. And the main tool of that power was violence. On big plantations, overseers were paid to squeeze out the biggest possible crop, and the whip was how they did it. Some owners went further, with branding, iron collars, and worse.

For enslaved women, there was another kind of violence. Owners had near-total control over their bodies, and many used it to rape them. Under the law, it wasn't even a crime.

In 1850, a Missouri farmer named Robert Newsom bought a girl named Celia. She was 14. For the next five years, he raped her again and again. She had children.

In 1855, pregnant again, Celia told him to stop. He didn't. So the next time he came to her cabin, she hit him with a heavy stick, and he died.

At her trial, her lawyers made a simple argument. Missouri law said a woman could use deadly force to protect herself from rape. The judge ruled that law didn't apply to her. In the eyes of the court, Celia wasn't a woman defending herself. She was property that had turned on its owner.

Celia was hanged in December 1855. She was about 19 years old.
