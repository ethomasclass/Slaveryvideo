So let's go back to Southampton County.

Nat Turner was born there in 1800. Even as a kid, the people around him thought he was special. He learned to read so young he couldn't remember learning. As an adult, he preached to other enslaved people, and some of them called him the Prophet.

He also had visions. He described "white spirits and black spirits engaged in battle," the sun going dark, and blood flowing in streams. He came to believe God had chosen him to fight slavery. So in February 1831, when the moon slid in front of the sun in the middle of the day and the sky went strange and dim, he took it as the signal. When the sun turned a strange bluish-green that August, he took that as the second.

On August 21st, he met his followers in the woods. That night, they began. They killed nearly every white person they found, including women and children. Then came the revenge, and the killing of Black people who'd had nothing to do with it.

Turner hid in the woods for more than two months. On October 30th, a farmer found him.

In jail, a white lawyer named Thomas Gray interviewed him and published The Confessions of Nat Turner. We have to read it carefully, because Gray chose what to print. But in it, Turner doesn't apologize. When Gray asked if he now saw he'd been mistaken, Turner answered with a question: "Was not Christ crucified?"

Nat Turner was hanged on November 11th, 1831. Virginia hanged others too, and sold still others out of the state.

He fought a system that was violent every single day. His rebellion also killed children. Historians still wrestle with how to hold both of those facts at once. You probably will too.
