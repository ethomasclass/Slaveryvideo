February 12th, 1831. Southampton County, Virginia.

In the middle of the day, the moon slides in front of the sun, and the sky goes strange and dim.

Most people in the county see an eclipse. One man sees a message from God.

His name is Nat Turner. He's 30 years old. He's enslaved. He can read, which almost no enslaved person around him can. And he preaches. For years, he's been waiting for a sign.

Six months later, on the night of August 21st, he acts. Turner and a handful of enslaved men move from farm to farm, killing the white families inside. Their numbers grow to dozens. By the time armed white men stop them, about 55 white people are dead.

What comes next is worse. White mobs and militias kill Black people across the region, many of whom had nothing to do with the revolt. And Turner disappears into the woods.

Then Virginia does something nobody expected. In the state capitol, lawmakers stand up and openly debate ending slavery.

They vote no. And then they make it harsher.

So here's the question I want to answer today: Why did the South, at the very moment it could have started letting slavery go, hold on tighter than ever?
