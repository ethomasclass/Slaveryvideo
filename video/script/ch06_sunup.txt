So what was life like for the people the whole pyramid stood on?

On a cotton plantation, most enslaved people worked in the fields, in groups called gangs, from sunup to sundown. At harvest, even longer.

At the end of every day, each person's cotton was weighed. Fall short, and you could be whipped. Hit your number, and your number might go up.

Women worked the same hours as men. Pregnant women were sent to the fields until close to giving birth, then sent back within weeks, often carrying their babies with them. Children started working at five or six years old: carrying water, pulling weeds, chasing birds away from the crops.

Food was a weekly ration, mostly cornmeal and a little pork. Clothing was a couple of rough outfits a year and one pair of shoes. Families slept in small wooden cabins, often with dirt floors, sometimes two families to a room, on beds of straw and rags.

Some enslaved people worked in the owner's house instead. They often got better food and hand-me-down clothes. But better is a relative word. Working in the house meant living under the owner's eye, day and night. As a boy, Frederick Douglass started learning the alphabet in a house in Baltimore, until the man of the house found out and stopped it. Reading, the man said, would make a boy unfit to be a slave. Douglass decided that was exactly why he had to learn.

And for an enslaved woman, being close to the owner could be the most dangerous place of all.
